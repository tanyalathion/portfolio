## Context

🎯 **Target:** Prospective students looking for flexible studies, researchers joining a recognized institution, plus the general public and media following the accreditation process.

😖 **Pain:** The existing website mirrored the internal org chart. How online studies work was barely visible, academic research was underrepresented, and the three language versions didn't always say the same things in the same way.

📱 **Solution:** A new information architecture built around visitors rather than departments, and a writing guide to keep content consistent across three languages.

🚀 **Approach:** Stakeholder alignment first, then content audit, architecture exploration and validation with real visitors. Carried out with the UX agency 8ratio, with me as Product Owner on the institute side.

## Methodology

A full redesign touches every department, so the first risk wasn't the navigation: it was misalignment. I chose to start with the people before the pages, then let visitors arbitrate between architecture options rather than internal opinions.

The project followed three steps: align, structure, sustain.

## 🤝 Align: build a shared vision of the stakes

👉 **Workshops:** Two workshops with heads of different departments, facilitated with the agency. Instead of collecting opinions in silos, they created a space for dialogue between teams that rarely sit at the same table.

👉 **Audiences and needs:** Each group made explicit what our target audiences expect, and named its own needs and wishes for the new site.

👉 **Outcome:** Every stakeholder left with the same reading of the stakes and a clear view of what a full redesign implies. A site built by everyone, for everyone.

## 🧭 Structure: make navigation intuitive for very different audiences

👉 **Content audit:** We went through the entire existing content, assessed its gaps, and looked at how other universities and training institutions organize theirs.

👉 **Architecture options:** Several architecture options were illustrated, each expressing a different philosophy of grouping content. They served as arguments to discuss the relevance of each grouping and sharpen our shared understanding of the content.

👉 **Tree testing:** The option preferred internally was validated with **100 visitors**, asked where they would find **10 key pieces of content** in the new navigation.

👉 **Labeling:** For two important sections, visitors were also invited to suggest labels. A way to make sure the site's vocabulary speaks to those who read it, not only to those who write it.

## ✍️ Sustain: useful, consistent and durable content in three languages

📖 **Web writing guide:** A complete reference for every UniDistance Suisse contributor: web vs. print writing, tone and clarity, typography, microcopy, media, SEO, creation and validation process.

🌍 **Multilingual:** The guide accounts for the specifics of a trilingual university institute (French, German, English) and the communication needs of each audience. Consistency doesn't mean uniformity.

## 🚀 Implementation: from strategy to migration

With this strategy in hand, the communication team started migrating content to the new structure.

But when a concept meets implementation, reality always pushes back: unanticipated questions, things to validate with visitors, key pages to bring to life. We set up an ongoing consulting subscription with the agency so the team can get expert input at its own pace, and carry the project through without delays or surprises.

---

## 💡 Main learning

**Architecture is a political object before it is a navigation.** A site structured like an org chart is the symptom of an organization talking to itself. Moving to a visitor-centered structure means each department accepts losing "its" menu item.

That's why the order mattered:
- 📌 Align stakeholders before showing a single mockup
- 📌 Let visitors settle debates that internal opinions can't
- 📌 Give contributors the tools to keep the structure alive after launch

The architecture is only as good as the content that fills it, and the content only as good as the people who write it.

## 📚 Related literature

**Information architecture**
Rosenfeld, L., Morville, P., & Arango, J. (2015). *Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond* (4th ed.). O'Reilly Media.
👉 The reference on organization, labeling and navigation systems, the three layers worked on in this project.

**Tree testing**
Spencer, D. (2009). *Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories*. Rosenfeld Media.
👉 Covers how to design, run and analyze sorting and findability studies, the method used to validate the navigation and labels.

**Content strategy**
Halvorson, K., & Rach, M. (2012). *Content Strategy for the Web* (2nd ed.). New Riders.
👉 Frames content as a system requiring governance and process, the logic behind the writing guide and its validation workflow.
